# Doc 09 — GED Path & Trade School Entry Requirements (Texas)

## 1. The Headline Reframe (read this first — it changes the rest of the doc)

Version 1.0 of this doc treated the standalone GED test's age floor (16/17/18) as *the* gate to trade school. On closer research, that's not quite right, and it matters a lot for "as early as possible."

**The GED is a credential for people who don't have a diploma from an accredited or private school.** Shiloh already has one of those in progress: Texas law treats his homeschool as a private school ([[03-academic-tracker-tx-compliance|Doc 03]] §1), and Thomas, as its administrator, has sole legal authority to decide when Shiloh has completed a bona fide secondary education and to issue him a diploma — with **no state-mandated age floor** on that decision, unlike the GED test itself. Texas explicitly recognizes this: a 1999 change to the Texas Administrative Code specifically added "successful completion of home schooling at the high school level" as satisfying the secondary-education requirement for admission to TWC-regulated trade/proprietary schools — listed as an alternative to, not dependent on, a GED.

**What this means concretely:** the fastest realistic route to trade training isn't "wait until Shiloh turns 17 and sit the GED" — it's "close the academic gaps, and once Thomas judges the record genuinely solid, issue the diploma and move straight to trade training," which isn't tied to a state-mandated birthday at all. The real remaining gates are (a) whatever minimum age the *specific trade program or apprenticeship* sets on its own — often 16, sometimes 18 — and (b) doing this rigorously enough that the diploma is a credible, defensible record and not just a shortcut, which is the same standard [[03-academic-tracker-tx-compliance|Doc 03]]'s evidence log already exists to meet.

The GED still has real value as a *second, independent, universally-recognized credential* (Section 6) — it's worth keeping in the toolkit rather than discarding — but it should be understood as a backup/supplement, not the only door.

## 2. Standard GED Route — Timing (unchanged from v1.0)

If the GED test itself is the route pursued, Texas requires test-takers to be 18, with narrow exceptions:

- **Age 16:** only if in state agency care, under a court order requiring/allowing testing, or in a Job Corps program (see Section 4) — not a fit for Shiloh's situation today outside of the Job Corps route specifically.
- **Age 17:** allowed with (a) government-issued photo ID, (b) Texas residency, (c) not currently enrolled in an *accredited* high school, (d) parental/guardian consent, and (e) no prior high school diploma. Processing the eligibility paperwork through TEA's help desk (non-enrollment verification, consent form) runs about 2–3 business days.
- **Age 18:** no restrictions.

If Section 1's diploma route is used instead, this age gate simply doesn't apply — it only governs the standalone test.

## 3. What the GED Actually Tests

Four subject tests, each scored independently on a 100–200 scale:

| Subject | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Reasoning Through Language Arts (RLA) | Reading comprehension, grammar/editing, a short evidence-based essay |
| Mathematical Reasoning | Arithmetic through basic algebra/geometry; a portion allows a calculator |
| Science | Reading/interpreting scientific text, data, and short scenarios (life, physical, earth science) |
| Social Studies | Reading/interpreting civics, US history, economics, and geography material |

**Passing bar:** a scaled score of **150+ on each of the four subjects independently** — a strong score on one can't cover a weak score on another. Score bands: 100–144 below passing, 145–149 "close" (this is where the free GED Ready practice test is most useful), 150–164 passing, 165–169 "college ready," 170–200 "college ready + credit."

The practical read: this maps onto exactly what [[01-holistic-quadrants-the-circle|Docs 01]]/03 are already building — real reading comprehension, functional writing, algebra-readiness math, and civics literacy. No separate GED-specific curriculum is needed years in advance.

## 4. Cost & Prep

- **Official test fee:** roughly $30–$40 per subject, ~$120–$160 total if each is passed on the first attempt.
- **GED Ready (practice test):** ~$6–$7 per subject — a useful early calibration tool, and not age-gated the way the real test is (see Section 6).
- **Free prep:** GED.com's own free practice questions and study guides; Texas adult-education programs (TCALL, 800-441-7323) offer free classes and tutoring, though some seats are earmarked for adult (21+) learners.
- No class attendance is mandated in Texas — testing is by appointment at a test center or, for some subjects, online with a parent present for check-in.

## 5. What Trade School / Job Training Actually Requires

The GED **and** a homeschool diploma are both treated as fully equivalent to a public diploma for admissions purposes.

**Typical admission requirements** (private trade schools and community-college technical/workforce programs alike):
- High school diploma **or** GED **or**, per Texas TAC, documented completion of homeschooling at the high school level
- Minimum age 16–18 depending on the specific program — this is the gate that actually matters most now that Section 1's reframe is in play. It's set by the individual school or apprenticeship sponsor, not by state credential law, so it has to be checked program-by-program.
- Parental/guardian consent if under 18
- An application, sometimes a fee, occasionally a placement exam or interview — most trade programs don't require SAT/ACT-style entrance exams

**The TSI wrinkle (Texas-specific):** community colleges require the TSI Assessment unless exempt. A GED score of 165+ on RLA and Math exempts a student entirely; separately, students enrolled in a Level 1 workforce/technical certificate taking *only* courses in that certificate plan (which covers most single-trade programs) are TSI-exempt regardless of score. For a straight trade-certificate track, TSI is unlikely to be a real obstacle either way.

**Typical program shape:** community colleges (Houston CC, Tarrant County College, El Paso CC, etc.) and private schools (e.g., Austin Career Institute) both offer trade certificates, usually well under two years — some single-trade certificates in 6.5–10 months. Roughly 260 two-year/postsecondary trade schools statewide. Average total cost cited around $17,450, varying enormously by trade and school type.

## 6. Getting There Faster — Realistic Early Options

This section is the direct answer to "as early as possible." Four routes, roughly ordered from most conventional to most aggressive, none of which are mutually exclusive.

### Option A — Dual-credit trade/CTE coursework while still homeschooling (potentially the earliest real start)

This is the option most people planning a "GED then trade school" sequence overlook, and it may beat every other option on pure timing. Texas homeschoolers have guaranteed legal access to dual credit, the same as public-school students, through community colleges — and it's available starting in **9th grade**, meaning it could realistically start around **age 14–15**, years before any GED or diploma question is even live.

Community colleges across Texas (Austin Community College is a documented example) run **Career Academies** — real workforce/CTE tracks in Welding, HVAC, Automotive, Construction, Health Sciences, Advanced Manufacturing, Information Technology — available to high-schoolers (including homeschoolers) through dual credit, often at waived or heavily discounted tuition (ACC waives tuition for up to 60 credit hours for eligible homeschoolers in its service area). A student can walk out of this with an actual workforce certificate, sometimes before finishing "high school" at all.

**The catch:** ACC's own policy requires the same college-readiness benchmark for dual credit as academic courses — roughly ACT 23 composite (19+ English/Math), SAT 480+ EBRW/530+ Math, or STAAR English III/Algebra II at the proficient (4000+) level. That's a real academic bar, not a formality, and it's *higher* in some ways than the bare GED pass line. Other Texas community college districts (Dallas College, Lone Star College, Tarrant County College) may set different thresholds — this is worth checking directly against whichever CCD serves the area once it's relevant, since policy varies by district.

**Why this matters for the plan already in motion:** this is one more concrete reason [[03-academic-tracker-tx-compliance|Doc 03]]'s gap-closing work is worth taking seriously *now* rather than later — hitting a solid Algebra II/English III STAAR-proficient level by, say, age 15–16 doesn't just support a GED or a traditional diploma, it's a literal ticket to starting real, paid-adjacent trade coursework years earlier than either credential route allows on its own.

### Option B — The homeschool-diploma route (Section 1), paired with a program that accepts it

Once Thomas judges the record solid — using [[03-academic-tracker-tx-compliance|Doc 03]]'s evidence log as the actual backing — he can issue a diploma with no state-mandated age floor. The gate becomes whichever specific trade program or apprenticeship is targeted, and their own age minimum. This needs a call to the target program to confirm they'll accept a parent-issued homeschool diploma without friction (most will, per Texas law protecting homeschoolers from discriminatory treatment — Doc 10 §2 — but it's worth confirming per-program rather than assuming).

### Option C — Registered apprenticeships (age 16 minimum for some trades)

Some Texas trade licenses allow apprentice registration as young as **16**. Electrician apprenticeship through TDLR is a documented example: minimum age 16, ~$20 registration fee, then several years of paid on-the-job hours (roughly 8,000 hours / 4–5 years) toward a journeyman license. Sources are mixed on whether a diploma/GED is a hard TDLR requirement to *register* as an apprentice versus something individual employers screen for when hiring one — this is worth a direct call to TDLR (electricians) or the relevant licensing board for other trades before counting on it, since the answer likely varies by trade and by employer.

**The general shape across Texas registered apprenticeships:** they combine paid work with formal training from day one — this is "get paid to train" rather than "pay tuition then get paid later," which is a real advantage over classroom-only trade school if the fit is right. Some registered apprenticeships are also Pell-Grant-eligible.

### Option D — Job Corps (federal program, combines GED/diploma completion *with* trade training, from age 16)

Job Corps is a free, federally-funded residential (or non-residential, at some centers) program specifically for **16–24 year olds**, income-eligible, that does not require a GED or diploma to enroll — it helps students **earn one concurrently** while training in a trade. It covers housing, meals, medical/dental care, and a biweekly living allowance, with programs running roughly 8 months to 3 years depending on the trade. Trades commonly offered include construction (bricklaying, carpentry, cement masonry, etc.), and around 10 broader industry categories overall depending on the center.

**Two real caveats, both worth weighing honestly:**
1. **Income eligibility** — Job Corps serves economically disadvantaged youth; household income (or Shiloh's own, if not claimed as a dependent) would need to qualify. Worth a direct eligibility check when the time comes rather than assuming either way.
2. **Program stability has been genuinely rocky.** In 2025, the Department of Labor moved to pause and close numerous Job Corps centers nationwide — including Texas's long-running Gary Job Corps Center in San Marcos, which closed after roughly 60 years of operation. Litigation and a February 2026 congressional appropriations act have since restored funding "through at least June 30, 2027" and blocked further widescale closures for now, and centers including North Texas Job Corps Center currently appear open and enrolling. But given the volatility over the past year, this is not a program to build a firm multi-year plan around without checking its live status again closer to whenever Shiloh would actually be eligible (age 16, i.e. around 2028).

## 7. What This Means for the Plan Right Now (age 14, Middle School Completion Year)

- **Nothing changes about this year's plan, and the timeline just got more flexible, not less.** Section 6 shows there isn't one fixed age gate for "starting real trade work" — there are several routes with different floors (14–15 for dual-credit CTE coursework contingent on readiness scores; 16 for some apprenticeships and Job Corps; whenever-Thomas-judges-it-ready for the homeschool-diploma route; 17/18 for the standalone GED). All of them still run through the same underlying requirement: solid reading, writing, math, and follow-through — exactly what [[03-academic-tracker-tx-compliance|Doc 03]]'s gap-closing plan is already building.
- **The most concrete near-term opportunity worth flagging to Shiloh, if a trade interest firms up:** the STAAR/SAT/ACT college-readiness benchmarks that gate dual-credit CTE coursework (Option A) are a specific, checkable target — hitting Algebra II/English III proficiency by 15 or so opens the door to real trade coursework years before any credential conversation is even necessary. Worth treating as a milestone in [[04-student-dashboard|Doc 04]]'s tracking once (and if) a trade interest is confirmed via the Five Audits ([[07-north-star-life-path|Doc 07]] §3).
- **This is still a two-way door.** None of the four options in Section 6 close off the traditional/college path (Doc 10) — dual-credit CTE hours, in particular, often count toward both a trade certificate *and* a high school transcript simultaneously, which is about as reversible and low-risk as a "bet" gets.
- **When a trade interest becomes real and specific:** the actual next action is a phone call, not more research — calling the specific target program (community college CTE office, apprenticeship sponsor, or Job Corps center) to confirm their current age floor, credential requirements, and (for Job Corps) live operating/funding status, since all three of those move faster than a reference doc can track.
