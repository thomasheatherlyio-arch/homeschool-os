## 1. Why This Fits This System

- **History and civics are easier to care about when they are a game you are already in.** Shiloh asked to learn history, civics, and society by playing *Civilization VI*. That is the same move [[kitchen-course|Docs 14]]–16 and [[forge-course|Doc 18]] already make: route required learning through a real interest, then demand evidence so nobody has to take "he learned it" on faith ([[00-governing-charter-and-toc|Doc 00]] Principle 3).
- **Good Citizenship is a Texas-required subject, and History / Social Studies is on the middle-school completion checklist.** [[03-academic-tracker-tx-compliance|Doc 03]] §4 needs both before a high-school year can start (target 9th grade: Fall 2027, pending Thomas's confirmation in [[hs-readiness]]). This course is built to produce evidence toward those two rows — constitutions, rights, governments, and lived civic practice, not just a high score on a map.
- **The game is a workshop, not the textbook.** Every unit pairs a specific stretch of play (a civ, an era, a mechanic) with real-history reading and a short written or spoken output. A recurring **Game vs. Reality** critique is required, because the game flattens conquest, leaves land looking empty, and lets every civ start in 4000 BCE. Noticing that gap *is* the academic work.
- **A Decision Log turns "I played for an hour" into proof of thinking.** Same-day entries in [[campaign-1-log]] (from [[civ-decision-log-template]]) record decisions and *why*, not just what happened. Era-change **Civ checkpoints** turn those choices into Socratic questions COLT asks and Shiloh answers — evidence Thomas can check.
- **Civics has to leave the screen.** At least two real-world actions are built in: interview Dad or a Knight, and attend or watch a city council meeting *or* visit a museum or the Texas State Capitol. Play sessions are time-boxed (45–60 minutes) and **only count when the written output exists**.
