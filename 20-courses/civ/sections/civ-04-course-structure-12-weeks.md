## 4. Course Structure — Unit 0 + 12 Weeks

Suggested pace: **Unit 0 first** (a few sessions, not skipped), then **one unit per week**. Campaign 1 ([[campaign-1-log]]) runs alongside the weekly units. A short second game is optional if a unit needs a specific civ and Campaign 1 is in a different era.

**Screen-time guardrail:** play sessions are **45–60 minutes**. A session counts as course work **only** when a Decision Log entry is written the same day. COLT's job in tutoring is to ask, then hint, then explain — never to answer the Socratic questions or write the output for Shiloh.

If an expansion is not installed, skip *Gathering Storm* items (World Congress, grievances, Diplomatic victory) or *Rise and Fall* items (loyalty) and use the base-game equivalent. Note that in the log.

### Unit 0 — How the Game Teaches History (onboarding; not skipped)

**Civ6 play.** Play one 45–60 minute session after (or while) going through the victory types below. Focus on early strategy, not on "winning this hour": where you settle, what you research, whether you start a district, and what a eureka or inspiration actually is. If war happens, log it — don't restart to pretend it didn't.

**Victory types (write each in your own words; COLT may quiz, not dictate):**

- **Science** — win by advancing through the science tree and completing the space / exoplanet race (the exact final steps depend on your edition).
- **Culture** — win by attracting enough incoming tourists through culture, tourism, and great works.
- **Religious** — win by converting every other civilization to your religion.
- **Diplomatic** — win with Diplomatic Victory points (World Congress, aid, and related actions). *Gathering Storm* only; if you don't have it, say so in the write-up and cross this one off as "not in my edition."
- **Domination** — win by capturing every other civilization's *original* capital. This is a real victory type and a real pattern in history. It is **not** the win condition for Campaign 1.
- **Score** — if the timeline runs out, the highest score wins. What does the game choose to count as "score," and what might that leave out?

**Early strategy basics to try, then log:**

- **Settling** — fresh water, resources, coast, distance from other cities. Why *this* tile?
- **Districts** — Campus, Theater Square, Holy Site, Commercial Hub, Industrial Zone, Encampment, Harbor. What does adjacency mean, and why would you build one of these *before* another army?
- **Eurekas and inspirations** — boosts on the technology and civics trees. Which boost did you unlock, and what real-world "we figured it out because we tried something" story might that be standing in for?
- **Choosing a victory path** — pick one **non-domination** path for Campaign 1 that fits your leader, your start, and what you actually want to get better at. You can still fight wars.

**War as a history lesson, not a ban.** Armies, declarations of war, warmongering penalties, **loyalty** (*Rise and Fall*), and **grievances** (*Gathering Storm*) are part of the course. When you go to war, the log should be able to answer: what did it cost (production, amenities, opportunity — what didn't get built)? What might a real people have paid that the game barely shows?

**Socratic prompts (do not look up "the" answer in this file — there isn't one here):**

- If the only way you have fun is declaring war, what would have to be true about a Science or Culture path for it to stay interesting for a whole age?
- The game lets you "win" history. Real history doesn't have a single winner. What is the game training you to care about, and should you trust that?
- You already know how to click units. What is a *strategy* decision you made this session that is different from a *mechanic*?

**Game vs. Reality.** Every civ in this game starts in the Ancient Era with a settler. No real civilization works like that. What else did you notice that is convenient for a game and suspicious as history?

**Deliverable.** A one-page write-up: the six victory types in your own words, the non-domination path you pick and why, and your first [[campaign-1-log]] entry. Show Dad. Pending until he verifies.

### Week 1 — River Valleys & Early Civilizations

**Civ6 play.** Play as **Egypt**, **Sumer (Gilgamesh)**, or **China**, or study Campaign 1's opening through this lens. Mechanics: settling on a river or floodplain, growing a capital, early tiles and workers/builders, the first district.

**Reading.** Khan Academy World History (early humans / river-valley civilizations) and/or Crash Course World History on Mesopotamia, Egypt, or the Indus. See [[civ-12-sources|Sources]].

**Socratic prompts:**

- Why did so many early states form next to rivers — and what happens in the game if you *don't* settle that way?
- Who actually does the farming in a real river-valley society? Does the game make that labor visible?
- If you "found" a city in one turn, what real process is being skipped?

**Game vs. Reality.** The map often looks empty except for barbarians and a few city-states. What does that hide about the people who already lived on that land?

**Deliverable.** An annotated map (hand-drawn or screenshot with notes) of your start plus a short Game vs. Reality reflection. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 2 — Greece: Democracy and Citizenship

**Civ6 play.** Play as **Greece** (Pericles or Gorgo), or spend the week's session on government and the early civics tree in Campaign 1. Mechanics: **Classical Republic** (or the earliest government you can adopt), the civics tree, city-state influence if it comes up, culture-adjacent districts.

**Reading.** A short, solid explanation of Athenian democracy (Khan Academy World History or Crash Course). You are comparing *Athens*, not "all of Greece," and not the modern United States — those come later.

**Socratic prompts:**

- Who got to speak and vote in Athens, and who did not? If the game's "Democracy" or "Classical Republic" card had to list those people, what would you write?
- Is a citizen the same thing as a resident? As a soldier? As a slave? As a woman in classical Athens?
- What is the difference between "my cities are happy" in the game and "the people have a say"?

**Game vs. Reality.** The game can let one leader flip a government in a single civic unlock. How does a real constitution or assembly actually change?

**Deliverable.** A short comparison: Athenian democracy vs. the government you adopted in-game. One paragraph minimum on who was left out in Athens. Evidence for Dad.

---

### Week 3 — Rome: Republic, Law, and Empire

**Civ6 play.** Play as **Rome** (Trajan), or look at roads, legions, and a government change in Campaign 1. Mechanics: early empire expansion, Roman roads (if available), military units, and *whether* you stay a republic.

**Reading.** The Roman Republic, citizenship, the Senate, and the shift toward empire (Khan Academy or Crash Course).

**Socratic prompts:**

- What did Roman citizenship give someone — and what did it cost the people who didn't have it?
- When does a republic start to behave like an empire? What signs would you look for in your own game?
- Roads, laws, and armies: which one is the game best at showing, and which one is it worst at showing?

**Game vs. Reality.** A "legion" is a unit you click. What was a real legion's relationship to politics, pay, and loyalty?

**Deliverable.** A timeline from Republic to Empire (key beats in your own words) plus a Game vs. Reality note on citizenship or the army. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 4 — Faith and Society

**Civ6 play.** Found or spread a religion: Holy Site, Great Prophet, beliefs. Arabia, Russia, Spain, or any civ in Campaign 1 that can still found or use a religion.

**Reading.** How major belief systems shaped law, community, and conflict — society, not a theology exam. Use Khan Academy World History on belief systems and/or a Crash Course episode on a tradition you actually encountered in game.

**Socratic prompts:**

- What does a "belief" *do* in the game (bonuses, pressure, victory)? What does a belief do in a real community that cannot be scored?
- Can you have a society with a religion and also have people who disagree? How does the game handle disagreement?
- If you "win" a Religious Victory, what has actually happened to everyone else's tradition?

**Game vs. Reality.** Great Prophets appear as named units. What is the game flattening about how religions actually start and spread?

**Deliverable.** A short reflection: one way religion shaped a real society you read about, and one way the game's religion system helps or misleads. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 5 — Trade and Wealth

**Civ6 play.** Build trade routes and traders; notice luxury and strategic resources, gold, and who you will (and won't) trade with. Strong fits: **Mali**, **Phoenicia**, **England**, or Campaign 1's commercial / harbor districts.

**Reading.** Historical trade networks (Silk Road, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan, or Mediterranean — pick the one closest to your game). Khan Academy World History.

**Socratic prompts:**

- Who gets rich from a trade route, and who does the hauling, the farming, or the mining?
- What happens to a city that has no "luxury" in the game vs. a real place left out of a trade network?
- If you embargo someone, is that economics, diplomacy, or a kind of war?

**Game vs. Reality.** A trader is a unit with a path. What risks (bandits, disease, monopoly, coercion) is the path leaving out?

**Deliverable.** A map of a real historical trade network next to a sketch or screenshot of your in-game routes, with a short note on the differences. Evidence for Dad.

---

### Week 6 — Middle Ages and Feudalism

**Civ6 play.** Enter or study the Medieval Era: the **Feudalism** civic, **Monarchy** (or whatever government you actually adopt), walls, knights, encampments. France, Japan, England, Georgia, or Campaign 1 mid-game.

**Reading.** Feudal obligations, manorial life, and (if you have time) why Magna Carta gets mentioned in later civics. Khan Academy or Crash Course on the Middle Ages.

**Socratic prompts:**

- In a feudal system, who owes what to whom? Can you draw that as a pyramid without leaving someone off the bottom?
- The game lets *you* be the ruler. Whose point of view is missing when "you" are the only decision-maker?
- What is a castle *for*, in real life, besides looking like a wonder?

**Game vs. Reality.** Unlocking "Feudalism" is a civic research. Was feudalism a technology someone invented, or a set of relationships that grew under pressure? What's your evidence?

**Deliverable.** A feudal-pyramid diagram plus a Game vs. Reality paragraph. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 7 — Exploration and Colonization (handle honestly)

**Civ6 play.** Renaissance-era navigation, overseas settlement, intercontinental trade. Spain, Portugal, England, Netherlands, Norway, or Campaign 1 if you are exploring. Mechanics: settlers abroad, new continents, trade, first contact, and (if present) grievances / loyalty after you take or found cities far from home.

**Reading.** Age of Exploration, the Columbian Exchange, and colonization — including **costs to colonized peoples**, not only the explorers' "discoveries." Khan Academy World History; Crash Course as a second source so you can compare.

**Socratic prompts:**

- The game often treats "unclaimed" land as empty. Who might already have been there, and what happens in real history when an empire calls that land empty?
- What moved in the Columbian Exchange besides gold and maps — people, plants, animals, disease? What does your game show, and what does it skip?
- If a city flips by loyalty or is captured, the game keeps going. What does not keep going for the people who lived there?

**Game vs. Reality (required and honest).** Name at least two things the game simplifies about colonization (for example: empty-looking land, conquest as a clean border change, little or no disease, "settlers" with no one already home). You are not being asked to apologize for playing; you are being asked to see the model clearly.

**Deliverable.** A written critique (one page or a tight two-paragraph minimum) of what the game leaves out, tied to a specific event in your session or a specific source you read. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 8 — Revolutions and the Founding of the United States

**Civ6 play.** Work the civics tree toward later governments (**Democracy** if you can reach it). Playing as America is optional and *not* required: in this game, "America" still starts in the Ancient Era with a settler, which is itself a Game vs. Reality prompt. The academic core this week is the **reading and the diagram**, not the leader choice.

**Reading.** U.S. Constitution, Bill of Rights, three branches, checks and balances (Khan Academy U.S. Government & Civics; iCivics Constitution / Bill of Rights units). Add a short Texas-government pass: governor, legislature, courts — Grade 7/8 Social Studies TEKS as the scope check (see Sources).

**Civic action (the first of two).** Interview Dad or a Knight: how does government actually touch their life (a law, a tax, a vote, a court, a local service)? Prepare three questions in advance. Log the interview date and what you learned — not a transcript of private family talk beyond the civic point.

**Socratic prompts:**

- Why split power into branches at all? What goes wrong if one person holds all of them — in the game, and in real life?
- A Bill of Rights limits government. Which right would you miss first if it were gone, and why?
- How is Texas government like the federal government, and how is it *not*? (Governor vs. President is a start, not the whole answer.)
- The game's "Democracy" policy card is a bonus. The real Constitution is a rulebook that also says *no*. Where do you see "no" in what you read?

**Game vs. Reality.** You can adopt "Democracy" without a convention, a ratification fight, or amendments. What work is the game skipping?

**Deliverable.** A diagram of the three federal branches (and a matching Texas sketch: governor, legislature, courts) plus a short mini-presentation to Dad. Interview notes count as part of the evidence. Pending until he verifies.

### Week 9 — Industrialization

**Civ6 play.** Industrial Era: Industrial Zone, railroads, factories, coal and other strategic resources, faster growth and pollution or amenities pressure if your edition shows it. Germany, England (Victoria), America, or Campaign 1.

**Reading.** The Industrial Revolution and its costs — labor, cities, environment, inequality — not only "more production." Khan Academy World History; Crash Course on industrialization.

**Socratic prompts:**

- Who paid for cheaper goods with longer days, dangerous work, or a changed landscape?
- The game rewards you for building factories. What would a factory worker's Decision Log entry look like, if they got to write one?
- Once you can produce more, do you *have* to expand, or is that just the habit the game rewards?

**Game vs. Reality.** An Industrial Zone is a district you place. A real industrial city also had unions, child labor debates, smog, and new kinds of wealth. Which of those can your game even represent?

**Deliverable.** A before/after comparison (two sketches, two screenshots, or a tight written contrast) of your civilization just before and after industrialization, plus one paragraph on a real-world cost the game underplays. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 10 — Ideologies and World Wars

**Civ6 play.** Modern / Atomic governments and policy cards: **Democracy**, **Fascism**, **Communism** (use the names your edition actually gives). Alliances, declared wars, warmongering, grievances, and World Congress (*Gathering Storm*) if available.

**Reading.** Why the world wars happened, and how ideology shaped the 20th century — causes and civic lessons, not a catalog of every battle. Khan Academy World History; Crash Course 20th century. Stay age-appropriate: study the stakes and the systems, not graphic content.

**Socratic prompts:**

- An "ideology" in the game is a set of policy cards. In real life, what else does an ideology demand of people besides a bonus?
- What made 20th-century wars different in *scale* from the wars you fought in the Ancient Era — and does the game show that difference honestly?
- If World Congress is in your edition, who is at the table, and who isn't? What real body is this standing in for, poorly or well?

**Game vs. Reality.** You can switch ideology when a civic completes. Real regimes rose and fell with parties, propaganda, fear, and resistance. Name one thing your war or your government change in-game was *too easy* compared to the reading.

**Deliverable.** A three-way ideology comparison chart (in your own words) plus a Game vs. Reality paragraph. Evidence for Dad.

### Week 11 — Modern Civics: Rights, Voting, and Local Government

**Civ6 play.** Late civics, late governments, and World Congress if you have it. The session can be shorter: the **civic action** is the center of this week.

**Reading.** Voting, rights and responsibilities of citizens, and how local government works (city council, mayor, school board, county). Khan Academy U.S. Government & Civics; iCivics local / state government resources.

**Civic action (the second of two — pick one, or both if you can):**

- Attend or watch a **city council** meeting (in person or the official livestream) and write what was decided, who spoke, and one question you still have; *or*
- Visit a history **museum** or the **Texas State Capitol** (tours: see Sources) and bring back notes or a photo you are allowed to take, plus one connection to a unit you already finished.

**Socratic prompts:**

- What is a right you have, and what is a responsibility that comes with it? Can you have one list without the other?
- If you were at the council meeting, whose job is it to listen to you, and what would you actually say?
- The game lets *you* pass policies instantly. After watching real local government, how long does a decision really take — and why might that slowness sometimes be a feature?

**Game vs. Reality.** There is no city council in *Civilization VI*. What parts of citizenship are therefore easy to forget if the game is your only teacher?

**Deliverable.** Civic-action notes plus a written reflection on rights and responsibilities. Evidence for Dad.

### Era-change Civ checkpoints (parallel to the weekly units)

These are triggered by **the game's era**, not by the week number. When Campaign 1 enters **Classical, Medieval, Renaissance, Industrial, Modern, or Atomic**, Shiloh says **"Civ checkpoint."** COLT reads [[campaign-1-log]] and asks **three** Socratic real-history questions **tied to choices already in the log** (a government adopted, a wonder started, a war declared, a religion founded, a deal signed, a district prioritized). COLT does not recycle the unit prompts above unless they genuinely match what Shiloh did. Answers are written down and filed as evidence, `verified: pending`.

There is no Future-era checkpoint required; if the game gets there, fold any last questions into the capstone.
