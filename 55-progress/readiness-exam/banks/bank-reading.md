## Passage A — "The Second Timer" (literary)

Maya set the kitchen timer for twenty-five minutes and slid the mixing bowl toward the center of the counter. Tonight's job was not a fancy dish. It was to test one sauce, write down what happened, and leave the kitchen cleaner than she found it.

The first batch broke. The oil separated, the pan smoked, and Maya's face got hot. She almost dumped the whole thing and texted her friend that the test was a bust. Then she looked at the card she had written before she started: *If it fails, write why. Then try once more.*

She turned the heat down, measured the oil more slowly, and started the timer again. The second batch came together — not perfect, a little too sharp with lemon, but it stayed in one piece. Maya wrote two lines in her notebook: "High heat broke the sauce. Lower heat, slower oil, still too much lemon." She underlined *still too much lemon* so her future self would not skip it.

When her dad came in, he asked if it had worked. Maya almost said yes, because the second batch looked fine. She showed him the notebook instead. "It worked the second time," she said. "And I know what I would change."

## Passage B — "Why Ranch Windmills Still Matter" (informational)

On many Texas ranches, a steel windmill still stands over a well, its fan turning whenever the wind picks up. The machine looks old-fashioned beside pickup trucks and electric pumps, but the idea behind it is simple and sturdy.

Wind pushes the fan. Gears change that spinning motion into an up-and-down stroke. A long rod, called the sucker rod, plunges into the well and lifts water into a storage tank. When the tank is full, a simple governor can throw the mill out of gear so the fan feathers and the pump rests. No app is required. No buried power line has to reach the pasture.

Ranch families keep some of these mills because the wind is already there on dry, open land, and because a well that can lift water during a power outage is a kind of insurance. The mills do need care. Bolts loosen. Wooden vanes crack. If the well is too weak, the mill can pump it dry. Still, a well-maintained mill can run for decades, quietly moving water from underground to a trough where cattle actually drink.

Engineers who study rural water systems often point out a trade-off: electric pumps are easier to start and stop on a schedule, but they fail when the power fails. A windmill fails when the wind dies or the hardware breaks. The best-kept ranches, those engineers note, usually keep more than one way to move water.
