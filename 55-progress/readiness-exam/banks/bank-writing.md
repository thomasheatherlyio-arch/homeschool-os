# Bank — Writing

**Status: draft** (pending Dad's approval of [[20261122-readiness-examiner]]). **Items: 12.** Scoring rubrics and sample features live only in [[key-writing]]. Do not open the key during a sitting.

**Strands:** writing process — plan, develop, revise (8.10A–C); genres — literary, informational, argumentative, correspondence (8.11A–D). Conventions are scored on longer pieces but are not the main focus of this bank (see [[bank-spelling-grammar]]).

Write in complete sentences unless an item asks for a list or outline. For timed sittings, spend the most time on W10–W12 (the longer prompts). A sitting may use a subset (for example W1–W4 plus one longer prompt) if 15–25 minutes will not cover all 12.

## Short items

### W1 (easy, constructed response) — TEKS 8.10A
You are writing a one-page explanation for a classmate who has never used a kitchen scale. Who is your **audience**, and what is your **purpose**? Then list three facts or steps you would include, in a sensible order.

### W2 (easy, constructed response) — TEKS 8.10B(i)
Put these four parts of an informational paragraph in the best order and label each one (opening, detail, detail, close):
- The timer also makes it easier to take a short break and come back.
- A twenty-five-minute timer can keep a homework session from drifting.
- Used this way, the timer is a tool for focus, not a race.
- For example, you might spend the whole block on one math skill and stop when the timer rings.

### W3 (medium, constructed response) — TEKS 8.10C
Revise the paragraph below so it is clearer and more specific. Keep the same main idea. You may add one concrete example.

> Doing stuff in the morning is good. It helps you. You should do it because it is important and then your day is better.

### W4 (medium, constructed response) — TEKS 8.11D
Write a short, polite email (5–8 sentences) to a coach or activity leader asking to move one practice by thirty minutes next Tuesday. Include why you are asking, a proposed time, and a clear question you need answered.

### W5 (medium, constructed response) — TEKS 8.10B(ii)
The sentence "The windmill is interesting" is too vague for an informational paragraph. Write two stronger sentences that could open a paragraph about ranch windmills. Each sentence should include at least one specific fact or image (you may use what you already know, or invent a clearly labeled example fact for this practice).

### W6 (medium, constructed response) — TEKS 8.11C
A school is deciding whether eighth graders should keep a weekly activity log (what they practiced, for how long). Write a **claim** of one or two sentences taking a clear yes or no position. Then list two reasons you would use, and one fact or example that would support the first reason.

### W7 (easy, multiple choice) — TEKS 8.10C / 8.10B(i)
Which change would most improve a draft whose paragraphs all start with "And then"?
- A. Make every sentence shorter than five words
- B. Add transitions that show order or cause (for example, "After that," "Because of this") and vary the openings
- C. Remove the conclusion so the piece ends suddenly
- D. Replace all periods with exclamation marks

### W8 (medium, constructed response) — TEKS 8.10A
You have 20 minutes to plan, not write, an informational piece titled "How I would run a 25-minute study block." Sketch a plan: audience, purpose, three sections with a working heading each, and one example or detail under each heading.

### W9 (hard, constructed response) — TEKS 8.10C
Here is a student's draft sentence: "The Constitution is important because of rights and stuff and the government can't just do whatever and also amendments." Revise it into two clear sentences. Keep the ideas (rights, limits on government, amendments) but drop the filler.

## Longer prompts (use the writing rubric in the key)

### W10 (hard, constructed response — informational) — TEKS 8.11B, 8.10B
Write an informational paragraph or two (about 150–220 words) explaining **one** real process you know well (for example: warming up before a workout, cooking one dish, setting up a game, or starting a homework block). Assume the reader has never done it. Include an opening, steps or parts in order, at least one caution or tip, and a close. Do not invent a process you have never seen; write from real experience.

### W11 (hard, constructed response — argumentative) — TEKS 8.11C, 8.10B
Write a short argument (about 150–220 words) for this prompt: **Should a weekly plan have at least one unscheduled "flex" block?** Take a clear position. Support it with at least two reasons and one concrete example. Include a brief opening and close. You may mention a counterargument in one or two sentences.

### W12 (hard, constructed response — literary / personal) — TEKS 8.11A, 8.10B
Write a short scene or personal narrative (about 150–220 words) in which a character (you, or someone you invent) has to try a task a second time after the first try goes badly. Show, don't just tell, one thing the character notices on the second try. Include a beginning, a turning point, and an ending that is more than "the end."
