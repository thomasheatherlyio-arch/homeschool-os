# Doc 10 — The College Path: Requirements, Commitment, Cost, and the AI Factor (Texas)

**Purpose:** Companion to Doc 09 (GED Path) and Doc 07 (North Star). Where Doc 09 grounds the GED/trade fork in facts, this doc does the same for the traditional high-school-diploma → college fork: what it actually takes to get there, what it costs, what it demands day to day, and — because Shiloh is choosing a field in the 2030s, not the 2010s — what's known so far about how AI is reshaping the payoff on different degrees. Reference material, not a decision. Per Doc 07 §5, the actual choice (or the choice to keep both doors open) belongs in the Fork Log, made with Thomas, when it's actually time to decide.

## 1. What "Finishing High School" Actually Requires in Texas

Texas homeschools are legally private schools (Doc 03 §1), so **Thomas, as administrator, has full authority to design Shiloh's diploma** — there's no state-mandated homeschool curriculum. But if the goal is a diploma that reads as credible to colleges and unlocks the strongest admission/scholarship track, it's worth building it against the same bar public-school students clear: the **Texas Foundation High School Program (FHSP)**.

| Track | Total Credits | What It Adds |
|---|---|---|
| **Foundation (base)** | 22 | 4 English, 3 Math, 3 Science, 3 Social Studies, 1 PE, 1 Fine Arts, 2 Languages Other Than English, 5 Electives |
| **Foundation + Endorsement + Distinguished Level of Achievement (DLA)** | 26 | Adds a 4th Math credit (through Algebra II) and 4th Science credit, plus completion of one **endorsement** (a specialization track) |

**The five endorsement tracks:** STEM; Business & Industry; Public Service; Arts & Humanities; Multidisciplinary Studies. An endorsement doesn't need to be locked in early — it's really just "which electives cluster together" and can follow whatever Shiloh's Interest Ledger (Doc 07 §3) turns up.

**Why DLA matters specifically:** it's the credential that qualifies a student for Texas's **Top 10% automatic-admission rule** at public universities (class-rank-based, so less directly relevant to a homeschooler, but the DLA distinction itself signals rigor to admissions readers regardless) and is generally treated as the "real" college-prep track by Texas public universities.

**The honest translation for right now:** 22–26 credits sounds enormous next to Doc 03's current "Middle School Completion Year," but it's spread across roughly four years (9th–12th) — the actual pace is about 6–7 credits per year, which is exactly the kind of steady, sustainable course load Doc 03 §6 is already designed around ("right-size the load," not "redo it harder"). No new pressure here — this table is just the map of where the current pace is headed if the traditional door is the one Shiloh eventually walks through.

## 2. What College Admission Actually Requires (Texas Homeschoolers Specifically)

The good news, stated plainly: **Texas law prohibits public universities from discriminating against homeschool applicants** — a homeschool diploma must be treated as equivalent to a public-school diploma, and the TEA does not accredit, monitor, or approve homeschool programs at all (Doc 03 §1). Nothing about being homeschooled closes a college door in Texas.

What admissions offices actually want to see from a homeschooler:

- **A transcript** Thomas builds and signs as administrator — courses, grades, credits, GPA. Not legally required to be notarized (public-school transcripts aren't either), but some schools request it; worth checking each target school's specific ask when the time comes.
- **A standardized test score** — SAT, ACT, or increasingly the CLT (Classic Learning Test). This carries more weight for homeschoolers than for public-school applicants specifically *because* there's no official class rank or accredited-school GPA context to compare against — it's the one data point admissions offices can benchmark against every other applicant nationally.
- **For community college / TSI purposes:** a placement exam (TSI Assessment, or the ACCUPLACER it's built on) unless exempt — see Doc 09 §4 for the GED-score exemption logic, which works the same way here for a diploma-track TSI Assessment score.
- **Documentation of the "soft" record** — extracurriculars, community service, leadership, work experience — which matters more than it used to (see Section 5 below on the "experience creep" finding).

**Dual credit is worth building into the plan early, not as an afterthought.** Texas law guarantees homeschoolers the same access to dual-credit courses as public-school students, through three channels: dual enrollment at a community college, AP exams, or CLEP credit-by-exam. Every dual-credit hour earned in high school is a college credit hour Shiloh won't have to pay tuition for later — this is one of the highest-leverage moves available on the traditional path, purely on cost grounds (Section 4).

## 3. What Getting Good Grades Actually Takes — the Honest Commitment Picture

This is the part most college-path conversations skip, and it's directly relevant given Doc 03's diagnosis of *why* 7th grade went sideways — so it's worth being precise about what the research actually shows, rather than assuming "just try harder" or "just study more."

**High school GPA predicts college success far better than test scores do — and it's not really testing content mastery.** A widely cited finding (from the *Crossing the Finish Line* research and confirmed by University of Chicago Consortium studies): a student with a 3.5 high school GPA but a mediocre ACT score is *nearly 20 percentage points more likely* to graduate college than a student with a strong ACT score but a GPA under 2.5. The reason: GPA is really a composite measure of exactly the executive-function skills Doc 01's fifth quadrant and Doc 03's gap-closing plan are built around — attention, note-taking, assignment completion, help-seeking, self-advocacy, and above all *time management*. This is a direct, research-backed validation of this whole project's actual bet: the process skill matters more than raw ability, for Shiloh specifically and for students in general.

**Raw study hours matter less than how the time is managed.** One frequently cited study found that each additional hour of weekly study time only nudged GPA up by about 0.025 points — meaningless on its own — while each one-point improvement on a standardized time-management-behavior scale correlated with a 0.3 GPA increase, roughly *ten times* the effect. The researcher's blunt conclusion: "a lifestyle change has to happen" before grades actually move; marginal effort increases don't do it. This again matches Doc 03's plan exactly — build the system first, load content second.

**What this means for the college path specifically:** the single best thing happening for Shiloh's college odds right now isn't a subject being studied, it's the Monday/daily/Friday planning rhythm (Doc 04) proving out over these next few years. A transcript full of A's built on a shaky planning system is a much worse bet for college survival than a transcript with solid B's built on a real, tested system Shiloh trusts under pressure — the second one is what actually walks into a dorm room and manages five unsupervised classes at once.

**Typical full-time college workload, for calibration:** research puts average weekly study time (outside class) around 15–20 hours for a full course load, on top of ~15 class hours — so a realistic ~30–35 hour "job" most weeks, higher during exam periods. Worth naming honestly now rather than as a surprise at 18: college is a full-time commitment, not a part-time add-on to a job or other pursuits, at least during a normal semester.

## 4. Cost of College and Loans — the Real Numbers

Figures below are national averages (most recent available, 2022–23 base year adjusted where noted) plus Texas-specific figures where they differ meaningfully.

| Path | Annual Cost of Attendance | 4-Year Total (incl. room & board) |
|---|---|---|
| Community college (in-district) | ~$17,400 | ~$34,900 (2 years) |
| **Texas public university, in-state** | ~$20,000 (tuition ~$8,200 + room/board) | **~$40,000/yr all-in → ~$160,000 for 4 years at full sticker price** (before any aid) |
| Public university, in-state (national avg) | ~$27,150 | ~$108,600 |
| Public university, out-of-state | ~$45,700 | ~$182,800 |
| Private nonprofit | ~$58,600 | ~$234,500 |

**Important caveat on "sticker price":** almost nobody at a private or many public universities pays the full listed figure — merit aid, need-based aid, and Texas-specific grant programs (like the TEXAS Grant for eligible in-state students) routinely cut the real number substantially. Sticker price is the ceiling, not the typical outcome, but it's the number worth planning around until real aid offers are in hand.

**Student loan reality, 2026:**
- National total student debt: **~$1.86 trillion**, ~91% of it federal.
- Average federal balance per borrower: **~$40,000**; bachelor's-degree holders average **~$29,550** in federal debt specifically (public-university bachelor's borrowers average closer to **$32,000**); associate's-degree holders average **~$20,300** — meaningfully less, one more point in favor of the community-college-first route below.
- **Current federal loan interest rates (2026–27):** Direct Subsidized/Unsubsidized (undergrad) **6.52%**, fixed for the life of the loan; Parent PLUS **9.07%**.
- **Federal borrowing caps for a dependent undergrad:** $5,500 (freshman) → $6,500 (sophomore) → $7,500 (junior+) per year, **$31,000 total aggregate**. This cap is a real constraint — it doesn't come close to covering full sticker price at most schools without either aid, family contribution, work income, or private loans (which carry no cap and often worse terms).
- **10% of federal student loan dollars were delinquent as of late 2025** — a real signal that a meaningful share of borrowers end up in a debt load their post-graduation income doesn't support. This isn't a reason to avoid college; it's a reason to be deliberate about *which* degree and *which* price tag, since both drive this risk far more than "going to college" as a category does.

**The highest-leverage cost move available, concretely:** starting at a Texas community college for the first two years (gen-ed/core-curriculum courses, which transfer directly under Texas's statewide core-curriculum agreement) and transferring into a four-year university for the final two, often saves on the order of $60,000–$80,000 versus four years at a public university, while ending in the exact same degree from the same school. Combined with dual-credit hours earned during high school (Section 2), it's realistic for Shiloh to enter a university as a junior having paid community-college or zero tuition for a third or more of the total credit hours a bachelor's degree requires.

## 5. The AI Factor — What's Actually Happening, Not the Hype Version

This is the piece that didn't exist as a real consideration for prior generations planning this fork, so it's worth being careful and non-alarmist about what the actual data shows versus what headlines claim.

**The current picture is more nuanced than "AI is taking jobs."** Since ChatGPT's 2022 launch, white-collar employment in the US has actually grown — roughly 3 million jobs added, software developer roles up ~7%, paralegal roles up ~21%, radiologist positions up ~10%, and real wages rising in both professional and administrative categories. Mass elimination of whole job categories has not been the dominant early pattern.

**The real early effect has a name: "experience creep."** Employers aren't broadly cutting entry-level hiring — one large employer survey found 46% actually *increased* entry-level hiring after adopting AI tools, versus only 13% who decreased it. What's changing is *what* entry-level work looks like: AI is absorbing the routine, formatting, and administrative tasks that used to be how new graduates learned the job, which leaves junior roles doing more analytical/judgment work sooner — but also means employers increasingly want candidates who already have some real experience, since there's less "easy" work left to hand a total novice. In one study, a graduate with a perfect GPA and zero experience ranked *last* among candidate profiles employers evaluated; candidates with internships or real project experience ranked first. **The practical implication for Shiloh, years out: real experience (internships, part-time work, actual built projects — small bets, in Doc 07's language) will matter at least as much as GPA for the first job after college, maybe more.**

**There's a real gap between what AI *can* theoretically do and what it *actually* does today.** Anthropic's own usage-pattern research (mid-2026) found AI can theoretically automate an estimated 94% of computer-and-math occupational tasks, but current real-world Claude usage covers only about a third of that — meaning the *ceiling* is high in knowledge-work fields, but the *current* displacement is well behind the theoretical maximum and closing gradually, not overnight. The same research found AI usage heavily concentrated in computer/math and management occupations (30%+ and 23%+ of usage respectively, versus their much smaller share of total employment) and nearly absent in physical, hands-on occupational categories — transportation, food service, construction.

**What both AI researchers and labor economists converge on as the more resilient combination of traits**, across multiple independent analyses:
1. **Physical presence plus real-time judgment** — work that happens in a variable physical environment where the "right call" depends on conditions no model was trained on (nursing, physical therapy, skilled trades, on-site engineering).
2. **High-stakes human trust and accountability** — regulated or consequence-bearing roles where a human has to be the one legally/ethically answerable (clinical care, licensed engineering sign-off, therapy/counseling, some legal and financial advisory work).
3. **Novel creative or strategic synthesis** — work that's genuinely new each time rather than "apply a known rule to structured data" (original design work, strategic leadership, research at the edge of a field).

**The clearest pattern in what's *most* exposed, across the WEF Future of Jobs report, Anthropic's data, and labor-market analyses alike:** routine, rules-based, structured-data work — data entry, basic bookkeeping, entry-level paralegal research, tier-1 customer support, template-based content/design, routine translation, cashier/clerical roles. The WEF's 2025 report specifically flags cashiers, administrative assistants, and accountants/auditors among the fastest-*declining* roles by 2030, while big-data specialists, AI/ML specialists, and software developers top the fastest-*growing* list — a reminder that "tech" isn't monolithically at-risk or monolithically safe; it depends heavily on whether the specific role is *building/directing* AI systems or *doing the routine work AI replaces*.

**One useful reframe worth naming for Shiloh directly, later, when this becomes relevant to him rather than to Thomas's planning:** the safest position by every framework above isn't really "pick an AI-proof major" as a static choice made at 18 — it's pairing a real domain (a trade, a science, healthcare, a craft) with genuine AI fluency as a *tool*, since virtually every resilient-field analysis above assumes the human in that role is working *with* AI systems competently, not competing against them from ignorance. This is worth folding into the North Star audits (Doc 07 §3) as Shiloh's interests firm up — not as a directive now, just as context worth having by the time a major actually gets chosen.

## 6. Degree Fields — Rough Resilience Read (Directional, Not a Ranking to Plan Around)

Treat this table as a starting lens, not a verdict — it's synthesized from multiple 2025–2026 analyses (Anthropic Economic Index, WEF Future of Jobs 2025, and several labor-market studies), all of which acknowledge real uncertainty about a fast-moving technology. Revisit it, don't memorize it.

| Directional Category | Example Fields | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **More resilient (for now)** | Nursing & allied health, physical/occupational therapy, skilled trades (electrical, HVAC, welding — see Doc 09), mechanical/civil engineering, cybersecurity, education (especially special ed), social work | Physical presence + real-time judgment, and/or high-stakes human accountability |
| **Resilient but changing shape** | Software development, AI/ML engineering, data science, architecture, design | High theoretical exposure, but currently growing because these fields increasingly mean *directing* AI tools rather than doing the routine work AI absorbs — the skill bar is rising, not disappearing |
| **More exposed** | Entry-level paralegal/legal research, bookkeeping/entry-level accounting, tier-1 customer support, data entry, template-heavy graphic design, basic copywriting/content editing, routine translation | Routine, rules-based work on structured data — the pattern flagged as most automatable across every source reviewed |
| **Genuinely uncertain** | Financial/investment analysis, general business/management, law (non-entry-level), general liberal arts | High theoretical automation potential per Anthropic's data, but current real-world impact still limited and highly dependent on specialization and seniority within the field |
