## 6. Deep Dive — Exercise Physiology & the Types of Fitness

### 6.1 The Three Energy Systems

Every movement is powered by turning stored energy into ATP (the molecule muscles actually burn). Which system dominates depends on how hard and how long the effort lasts:

| System | Duration | How it works | What it fuels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ATP-PC (Phosphagen) System** | ~0–10 seconds | Uses stored ATP and creatine phosphate directly — fastest energy, no oxygen needed | A single max lift, a sprint start, a vertical jump |
| **Glycolytic (Anaerobic) System** | ~10 seconds–2 minutes | Breaks down glucose without oxygen (anaerobic glycolysis) — fast, but produces fatigue byproducts | A hard set of 8–15 reps, a 400m sprint, a full-court press |
| **Oxidative (Aerobic) System** | 2+ minutes, sustained | Uses oxygen to break down carbs and fat — slower to fire up, but nearly unlimited fuel | A long run, a full basketball game's overall pace, general conditioning |

All three systems are always running at once — which one *dominates* just shifts with intensity and duration. This is the direct science behind why a powerlifter's training looks nothing like a marathoner's.

### 6.2 Muscle Fiber Types

| Fiber Type | Also Called | Speed/Power | Endurance | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Type I** | Slow-twitch, slow oxidative | Low force, slow contraction | High — fatigue-resistant | Posture, endurance activity, long aerobic efforts |
| **Type IIa** | Fast-twitch, fast oxidative ("intermediate") | Moderate force, moderate speed | Moderate | Mixed-demand sports — basketball is a strong example |
| **Type IIx** | Fast-twitch, fast glycolytic | High force, very fast contraction | Low — fatigues quickly | Max-effort lifts, sprints, jumps |

Everyone is born with a genetically set mix, but training shifts how those fibers perform — heavy strength work and explosive training preferentially develop Type II fibers; sustained aerobic work develops Type I capacity and mitochondrial density. Basketball, notably, trains all three systems and fiber types in the same game, which is part of why it's such a demanding sport physiologically.

### 6.3 Aerobic vs. Anaerobic — The Core Distinction

**Aerobic exercise** relies on oxygen to produce energy, recruits mostly Type I fibers, and is what builds cardiovascular endurance (running, swimming, cycling, a full basketball game's overall pace). **Anaerobic exercise** happens faster than oxygen delivery can keep up, relies on the ATP-PC and glycolytic systems, recruits mostly Type II fibers, and is what builds strength and power (lifting, sprinting, jumping).

### 6.4 The Major Training Disciplines — A Comparison

| Discipline | Primary Goal | Typical Method | Energy System Emphasis | Fiber Emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Powerlifting** | Maximal strength in 3 lifts (squat, bench, deadlift) | Low reps (1–5), heavy loads, long rest | ATP-PC | Type IIx |
| **Bodybuilding** | Muscle size (hypertrophy) and symmetry | Moderate reps (6–15), moderate rest, high volume | Glycolytic | Type IIa/IIx |
| **Calisthenics** | Relative strength and body control using bodyweight | Progressive skill/rep work (push-ups → dips → planche progressions, etc.) | Mixed — varies by move | Mixed |
| **Olympic weightlifting** | Explosive power (the snatch and clean & jerk) | Very low reps, technical, explosive | ATP-PC | Type IIx |
| **Endurance/aerobic training** | Cardiovascular capacity, stamina | Sustained effort over minutes-to-hours | Oxidative | Type I |
| **Functional/mixed training** (e.g., HIIT, CrossFit-style) | General fitness across strength, power, and endurance at once | Varied — circuits mixing loads, reps, and cardio | All three, in rotation | Mixed |

This table itself is the Unit 3 deliverable in expanded form — a great candidate for Shiloh to redo in his own words as the actual evidence artifact, rather than just reading this version.

## 7. Deep Dive — Workout Tracking Basics

Tracking is what turns "I worked out" into real, comparable evidence — the same "evidence over assertion" logic (Doc 00 Principle 3) that runs through this whole system.

**What to track, every session:**

| Metric | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Weight/load** | How much resistance was used | The core variable for strength progress |
| **Reps** | How many repetitions per set | Combined with load, shows total effort |
| **Sets** | How many rounds of that rep count | Part of total volume |
| **Volume load** | Weight × reps × sets, added up | The single best "am I actually progressing" number — e.g., 3 sets of 10 at 100 lbs = 3,000 lbs of volume that session |
| **RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion)** | A 1–10 self-rated effort scale | Tracks intensity even when weight isn't changing week to week |
| **Rest time** | Time between sets | Affects which energy system is being trained (Section 6.1) |

**Progressive overload** — the core principle behind all real progress — just means gradually increasing training difficulty over time (more weight, more reps, more sets, less rest, or better form at the same load) so the body keeps being forced to adapt instead of plateauing. A simple beginner rule: work up to the top of a rep range (e.g., 3×10) with good form, then add a small amount of weight and drop back to the bottom of the range, and repeat.

**Logging methods** — any of these work; the point is consistency, not the tool:

| Method | Good for |
|---|---|
| Physical notebook | Simplicity, no screens, satisfying to fill in |
| Spreadsheet | Easy to build formulas for volume load and track long-term trends |
| Note-taking app | Flexible, fast, always in his pocket |
| Dedicated workout-tracking app | Built-in graphs and automatic progress comparisons |

**Common mistakes to avoid:** logging inconsistently (a log with gaps can't show real trends), chasing heavier weight while letting form break down, and changing exercises too often to ever see whether progress is actually happening.

A working log template (ready to copy) is in Section 11.

## 8. Deep Dive — What It Takes to Become a Personal Trainer

*Researched August 2026. Requirements and pricing shift — reverify directly with the certifying body before Shiloh actually enrolls, especially once he's closer to 18.*

### 8.1 The Core Requirements

Every major certifying body (NASM, ACE, ISSA, ACSM, NSCA/CSCS) requires:

- **Minimum age 18** and a high school diploma or equivalent (GED-eligible per Doc 09).
- **Current CPR/AED certification**, required before sitting the exam — this has no meaningful age barrier and Shiloh could realistically earn this well before 18, as a real stretch-goal credential in its own right.
- **Passing a proctored certification exam.** Using NASM as the reference example: 120 questions, a 2-hour limit, and a scaled score of 70 to pass (NASM reports an 85% pass rate in 2025).

### 8.2 The Certification Landscape

| Certification | Approx. 2026 Cost | Accreditation Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **NASM-CPT** | $999–$1,399 | NCCA-accredited; widely recognized by gyms/employers |
| **ACE-CPT** | $989–$1,750 | NCCA-accredited; widely recognized |
| **ISSA-CPT** | ~$1,068 | NCCA-accredited exam available through a separate pathway (NCCPT) — worth confirming which package includes it |
| **ACSM-CPT** | $939–$994 | NCCA-accredited, research/clinical-leaning |
| **NSCA-CSCS** | $500–$1,000+ | NCCA-accredited; the standard for strength & conditioning coaching specifically (often paired with a bachelor's degree requirement) |

NASM and ACE are the two most consistently cited as the "gold standard" pairing for a gym-floor personal training career; CSCS is the discipline-specific choice if Shiloh's interest ends up leaning toward athletic performance/strength coaching rather than general personal training — worth revisiting once basketball-specific coaching interest (if any) is clearer.

## 9. Deep Dive — Flat Feet: What Shiloh Needs to Do

*Researched August 2026, general/educational information — not a medical diagnosis. If Shiloh hasn't had his feet formally evaluated by a podiatrist or physical therapist, that's worth doing before locking in a long-term corrective program, since severity and cause change what actually helps. This section is a research-informed starting point, and a genuinely good Unit 7 assessment case study — Shiloh is the subject and the researcher at once.*

### 9.1 What Flat Feet (Pes Planus) Actually Is

Flat feet means the medial longitudinal arch — the curve that normally runs along the inside of the foot — is collapsed or absent, so the sole sits closer to flat against the ground. Common causes include a weakened posterior tibial tendon (the most common acquired cause), underdeveloped intrinsic foot muscles, genetic ligament laxity, tight calves/Achilles tendons, prolonged wear of overly cushioned shoes, extra body weight, or a past ankle/foot injury.

### 9.2 Why It Matters Beyond the Foot Itself

The arch's real job is to act as a flexible shock absorber during walking, running, and jumping. When it collapses, the effects don't stay local — they travel up the whole kinetic chain:

- **Ankle:** overpronation (the foot rolls inward) and reduced ankle dorsiflexion, which limits how deep Shiloh can safely squat and changes how force moves through the ankle on every step or jump.
- **Knee:** the lower leg's internal rotation pulls the knees inward (valgus collapse) during squats, jumps, and cuts — exactly the kind of movement basketball demands constantly, and a real risk factor for knee injury over time.
- **Hip:** the same internal-rotation chain reaches the hip, contributing to poor hip mechanics and postural compensation.
- **Lower back:** the cumulative rotation up the chain can drive lower-crossed-syndrome-type posture and chronic lower back pain.

In plain terms: flat feet aren't just a foot issue for an athlete — they can quietly shape how the knees, hips, and low back move on every single step, jump, and landing.

### 9.3 The Corrective Plan

Conservative, exercise-based correction is the standard first-line approach (versus jumping straight to orthotics or surgery, which are reserved for pain that doesn't respond to exercise, or more severe/rigid cases). A phased plan, with real improvement typically showing up over **3–6 months of consistent work** — not days or weeks:

**Phase 1 (Weeks 1–4) — Wake the arch up:**
- *Short foot exercise* — standing or seated, try to "dome" the arch by pulling the ball of the foot toward the heel without curling the toes. 10 reps, hold 5 seconds, both feet.
- *Towel scrunches* — scrunch a towel toward you using only the toes. 2–3 sets of 10.
- *Toe yoga* — lift just the big toe while keeping the other four flat, then reverse; builds independent toe control. 10 reps each foot.
- *Tibialis posterior activation* — resisted foot inversion with a light band. 2–3 sets of 12–15.

**Phase 2 (Weeks 3–8) — Build real strength, overlapping with Phase 1:**
- *Single-leg calf raises, focused on the arch* — rise slowly, keeping weight through the big toe/second toe, not rolling to the outside edge. 3 sets of 10–12 per foot.
- *Heel walking* — walk 20–30 feet on heels only, toes lifted.
- *Marble/towel pickups* — pick up small objects with the toes, one at a time.
- *Single-leg balance with active arch engagement* — 3 rounds of 20–30 seconds per foot, doming the arch throughout.

**Phase 3 (Ongoing):**
- *Ankle dorsiflexion mobilization* (knee-to-wall stretch) — directly counteracts the tight-calf contributor to flat feet.
- *Plantar fascia release* — rolling the arch over a ball for 1–2 minutes per foot.
- *Barefoot training integration* — light, controlled barefoot work (on safe surfaces) to keep building intrinsic foot strength once the base work above is solid.

This whole protocol slots directly into the stretching routine below (Section 10) and the ongoing training log (Section 7) — it doesn't need to be a separate project, just a consistent 5–10 minutes worked into what Shiloh's already doing.

| # | Stretch | Targets |
|---|---|---|

### 10.3 Standalone Flat-Feet Session (3×/week, ~10 minutes, separate from training days)

## 20. Deep Dive — Fasting: Research & What Testing (If Any) Actually Makes Sense for Shiloh

*Researched August 2026. This section is written deliberately cautiously — fasting research on growing adolescents is thin, and the piece that does exist is a real yellow flag (Section 20.4). Nothing in this section is medical advice; anything beyond Section 20.6's Option A should go through Thomas and, ideally, a real conversation with Shiloh's doctor first.*

### 20.1 What "Fasting" Actually Means — The Menu of Protocols

"Fasting" isn't one thing. The common versions, from mildest to most aggressive:

| Protocol | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Overnight fast** | The normal gap between dinner and breakfast — everyone already does a version of this every night (roughly 10–12 hours) |
| **Time-restricted eating (TRE)**, e.g. 16:8 or 14:10 | All calories eaten inside a shortened daily window (e.g., noon–8pm), fasting the rest of the day |
| **Alternate-day fasting** | Normal eating one day, little-to-no food the next, repeating |
| **5:2** | Normal eating 5 days a week, very low calories (~500–600) on 2 non-consecutive days |
| **Extended fasting** | 24+ hours with no food, sometimes multiple days |
| **Religious fasting** (e.g., Ramadan) | A defined daily eating/fasting window tied to religious practice, studied fairly extensively in athletes |

### 20.2 What Happens Inside the Body During a Fast

As the fasted period lengthens, the body moves through a few distinct phases:

- **Hours 0–12 (roughly overnight):** the body runs on glucose from the last meal, then liver glycogen. Muscle glycogen — the fuel actually used during hard training — stays largely untouched at this stage.
- **Hours 12–24+:** liver glycogen depletes further, insulin drops, and the body shifts more toward burning fat for fuel ("metabolic switching"). Growth hormone tends to rise during fasting — one of the more interesting findings, though its practical benefit is debated.
- **Cellular level (autophagy):** fasting is believed to trigger autophagy — cells clearing out damaged components — a process linked in animal studies to some longevity and metabolic-health benefits. Human evidence for autophagy from realistic, everyday fasting windows (as opposed to extended multi-day fasts) is still an active, unsettled research area as of 2026.

### 20.3 What the Research Actually Shows — Adults & Athletes

- **Metabolic health (adults):** intermittent fasting shows real, replicated benefits for insulin sensitivity and some metabolic markers in adult studies — this is the strongest, most consistent finding in the research.
- **Muscle protein synthesis:** compressing eating into an 8-hour window (vs. 12) did **not** reduce muscle protein synthesis in one controlled study — but that study used sedentary, non-athletic adults eating at maintenance calories, and the researchers were explicit that results in trained athletes, or anyone in a calorie deficit, are still an open question. The practical takeaway that *did* hold up: protein needs to be spread evenly across whatever eating window exists — a short window makes hitting protein targets harder, not easier.
- **Strength & body composition:** in resistance-trained adults using 8-hour eating windows, studies found fat loss with muscle mass and strength generally preserved — but testosterone and IGF-1 (a growth-signaling hormone) dropped meaningfully in the fasting groups.
- **High-intensity performance:** Ramadan-style fasting research (the best-studied real-world fasting-and-athletics dataset) shows measurable costs to high-intensity output — reduced peak power in sprint testing, plus slower reaction time and attention, particularly late in the fasting window.
- **Bottom line for adults:** fasting is, at best, a *behavioral tool* for people trying to simplify eating patterns while managing calories — not a performance enhancer, and not obviously superior to just eating on a normal schedule at the same total calories.

### 20.4 The Critical Piece — Fasting and the Growing Body

This is the part that actually matters most for Shiloh specifically, and it's recent, real research: a 2025 study out of the Technical University of Munich and Helmholtz Munich found that **chronic intermittent fasting impaired insulin-producing pancreatic beta cells in young/adolescent mice**, while the same fasting pattern was beneficial in *adult* mice. Using single-cell sequencing, the researchers found the young beta cells "failed to mature properly" under fasting and developed defects resembling those seen in type 1 diabetes — while mice whose beta cells had already matured before fasting began were unaffected. The researchers' own conclusion: *"intermittent fasting is beneficial for adults, but it might come with risks for children and teenagers."*

That lines up with what registered dietitians who work specifically with teen athletes already recommend: **intermittent fasting is not currently advised for children or teens.** The reasoning holds up independently of the mouse study — teenagers are in a period of rapid growth and need consistent nutrient and calorie intake (this doc's own Section 8 already established active teen boys need ~2,800–3,200+ calories a day); restrictive eating windows conflict directly with that; and restrictive-eating patterns in teens carry a real, documented risk of tipping into disordered eating, which is already a heightened concern in this age group. The standard guidance for teen athletes runs the opposite direction from fasting: eat roughly every 3–4 hours, not less often.

### 20.5 What This Means for Shiloh, Specifically

Put plainly: **actual fasting protocols — skipping meals, compressing eating into a short window, or cutting calories on a schedule — are not appropriate for Shiloh right now.** He's 14, still growing, and training as an athlete with real caloric and protein demands (Section 8). That's true independent of the mouse study, and the mouse study is a specific, concrete reason to be extra cautious rather than treat this as a generic "everything in moderation" caveat. This is a genuinely good "study it, don't practice it yet" topic — the same posture Doc 09/Doc 10 take toward trade apprenticeships that don't legally open until 16 or 18.

### 20.6 What Safe "Testing" Actually Looks Like at 14

The instinct to *test* something rather than just read about it is exactly right for this system (Doc 00 Principle 3) — it just needs to be redirected from "restrict food" to "observe and measure," which is real science-method practice either way.

**Option A — recommended, no sign-off needed:** a pure **observation log**, not a fast. For one week, Shiloh logs his *natural* overnight fasting window (last bite at night → first bite in the morning), plus a 1–10 energy rating and a sleep-quality note each morning. No intentional restriction, no changed eating pattern — just honest data collection on a pattern that's already happening every night. This alone is a legitimate way to practice the actual research skill (forming a hypothesis, collecting data, reading results) without touching any of the risk factors in Section 20.4.

**Option B — only with Thomas's explicit sign-off, and ideally a conversation with Shiloh's doctor first:** a mild, **non-restrictive** 12:12 time-restricted window (e.g., last bite 8pm, first meal 8am) — which is barely different from a normal eating day — while still hitting full daily calorie and protein targets *within* that window. This tests eating *timing*, not eating *amount*, and stays close to what most people already do. Given Section 20.4, this should be treated as a real family/medical decision, not a default next step, the same real-world-decision gate Doc 11 §6 uses for anything with lasting consequences for a minor.

**Explicitly not recommended, at any point in this course:** 16:8 or longer daily fasting windows, alternate-day fasting, 5:2, or any protocol that reduces Shiloh's total daily calories or protein below the Section 8 active-teen targets. If personal interest in fasting persists, Section 20.3–20.4's findings suggest revisiting the question seriously at 18, when the actual research base (and Shiloh's own growth trajectory) looks different.
