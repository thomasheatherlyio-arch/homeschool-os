# Doc 16 — The Panel: Electrical Engineering & Electrician Career Course

**Purpose:** A 3-month (12-week), activity-based course introducing Shiloh to electrical engineering fundamentals and the electrician trade — built the same way Doc 14 (The Kitchen) and Doc 15 (The Gym) were: a real interest turned into a structured but self-paced curriculum that routes Texas-required and standard-practice subjects (Doc 03) through hands-on work, and doubles as a genuine Doc 10 Exposure Ladder candidate rather than a stand-alone unit sitting outside the rest of this system.

This course leads with something the other trade candidates in Doc 10 don't have yet: a live, well-documented, once-in-a-generation demand spike happening *right now*, driven by the AI data-center buildout — covered in full in Section 8. It also runs the full arc on purpose, from "what is voltage" (Week 1) to "what does it take to own an electrical contracting company with employees" (Section 10) — because the point isn't just to learn wiring, it's to see the whole ladder before deciding whether to climb it.

---

## 1. Why This Fits This System

Electrical work is unusual among the trades on Doc 10's menu because almost none of it can be learned by hand-skill alone — it's built on real math and real physics, which makes it an unusually strong vehicle for exactly the kind of interest-routed academics Doc 03 §6 calls for:

- **Every circuit is an algebra problem.** Ohm's Law (`V = I × R`) and Watt's Law (`P = V × I`) aren't abstract formulas here — they're the actual tool an electrician uses to size a wire or figure out why a breaker keeps tripping. Doc 09's existing GED-readiness math ("algebra-readiness math," "reasoning through language arts") gets a direct, motivated home.
- **Every panel is a systems-thinking exercise.** A house's electrical system is one interconnected system — service entrance, main panel, branch circuits, grounding — with safety margins built in on purpose (breaker sizing, wire ampacity). That's engineering thinking, not just trade knowledge, and it's the literal "basics of engineering" thread Thomas asked this course to carry.
- **Every code requirement is a civics lesson in disguise.** The National Electrical Code (NEC) exists because ungrounded, overloaded, or badly-installed wiring burns houses down and kills people — licensing (Section 9) exists to protect the public, not to gatekeep a trade. That's Doc 03's "good citizenship" subject, taught through something real instead of a textbook chapter.
- **The career ladder is unusually visible and well-documented.** Unlike a lot of career paths at 14, "apprentice → journeyman → master → business owner" is a real, numbered, publicly-tracked ladder in Texas (Section 9) — hours logged, licenses earned, income data by stage (Section 10). That kind of visible structure is exactly what Doc 07's Horizon Ladder needs to work with.
- **A finished, tested project is real evidence** — per Doc 00 Principle 3, a working circuit that Shiloh built and tested with a meter, and a real business-plan pitch backed by real numbers, are a paper trail nobody has to take on faith.

This also slots directly into Doc 10's existing Trade Menu (§3) — electrical is already listed there as a candidate, tied to the South Texas Electrical JATC and TDLR licensing. This course is what turns that one table row into a real, tested 3-month exposure.

## 4. Course Structure — 12 Weeks

Suggested pace: **one unit per week**, running as an afternoon/dedicated block (however many sessions fit the weekly dashboard — see Section 6). Units build on each other, but Shiloh can move faster through anything already comfortable (Section 5).

| Week | Focus | Key skills | Deliverable / evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Why Electricians, Right Now** | Orientation: what the trade actually is, the AI/data-center demand story, the full career ladder from apprentice to business owner | Reading the demand data, mapping the licensing ladder at a glance | A one-page written summary: "Why this trade, why now" |
| **2. Electrical Safety & Tools** | Jobsite and home-practice safety, PPE, lockout/tagout logic, tool ID (pliers, strippers, multimeter, non-contact voltage tester) | Safe practice habits, tool identification, reading a multimeter | A self-built safety checklist; a tool-ID worksheet |
| **3. Engineering Fundamentals I — The Physics of Electricity** | Voltage, current, resistance, power; Ohm's Law and Watt's Law; series vs. parallel circuits; AC vs. DC | Solving circuit-math problems by hand | A worked problem set; one hand-drawn series and one parallel circuit diagram, solved |
| **4. Engineering Fundamentals II — Systems Thinking** | How a house's electrical system works as one system (service entrance → panel → branch circuits → grounding); why engineers build in safety margins (breaker sizing, derating) | Systems diagramming, safety-margin reasoning | A labeled diagram of a home electrical system, built from research |
| **5. The Math of the Trade** | Wire-gauge (AWG) and ampacity, voltage-drop calculations, conduit-fill geometry, reading NEC tables | Applied algebra and geometry with real code tables | A completed wire-sizing/voltage-drop worksheet for a sample scenario |
| **6. Reading the Language — Blueprints & the NEC** | Electrical symbols, one-line diagrams, floor-plan electrical layers, navigating a code book/index | Technical reading and diagram literacy | An annotated sample blueprint; a short "how the NEC is organized" writeup |
| **7. Hands-On Circuits — Build & Test** | Building and testing simple circuits (series, parallel, switch-controlled) on a low-voltage trainer kit or breadboard; using a multimeter to measure voltage/resistance/continuity | Hands-on assembly, meter reading, troubleshooting | A built and tested circuit, photographed/logged, with meter readings recorded |
| **8. Residential Systems Case Study** | A real, unenergized walkthrough of a home's own panel with Thomas (breakers off, observation-only): identifying circuits, labeling, grounding, GFCI/AFCI locations | Real-world system reading, code-requirement application | A written circuit-labeling map of the home's actual panel |
| **9. The Licensing Ladder — Deep Dive** | TDLR's full license structure (Section 9), what each tier actually requires, and how Texas apprenticeships (e.g. JATC) actually run | Research, comparing pathways | A filled-in personal licensing roadmap (Section 9 template) |

## 5. Skill Placement — Start Where He Actually Is

Before Week 1 locks in, run a quick, honest self-check with Shiloh (COLT can run this conversationally) rather than assuming a level:

- **Brand new to electricity/electronics** → run the full 12 weeks at full length, with Thomas directly supervising every hands-on session (Weeks 7–8 especially) until comfort is demonstrated, not assumed.
- **Comfortable with basic math (algebra) but new to circuits** → Weeks 3–5 can move faster; spend the extra time on Weeks 7–8's hands-on work instead.
- **Already curious/tinkering with electronics at home** → compress Weeks 2–3, and let him lead more of Weeks 9–11 (research, outreach) independently, with Thomas checking the final plan rather than co-planning it.

**One rule that doesn't flex regardless of skill level:** this is a *learning-fundamentals-and-low-voltage-practice* course, not a live-household-wiring course. All hands-on circuit work (Week 7) uses a low-voltage trainer kit, battery-powered components, or a breadboard — never energized household current (120V/240V). Real household wiring work is licensed, dangerous, and legally restricted for good reason (Section 9) — that boundary is itself part of the lesson, not a limitation of the course. Week 8's panel walkthrough is observation-only, breakers off, Thomas present.

## 8. Why Now — The AI/Data-Center Demand Boom

*Researched August 2026 — figures below can shift; reverify anything decision-critical closer to when it matters.*

This is the piece Thomas specifically wanted front and center, so it's worth its own section rather than a single table row.

**The core driver:** the AI buildout runs on data centers, and data centers run on an enormous amount of electrical infrastructure — power distribution, cooling systems, backup generation, all of it built and maintained by electricians. Five major tech companies are collectively spending roughly **$700 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026** — nearly double their 2025 spending — and almost none of that money can turn into a working data center without electricians on-site.

**The shortage, in real numbers:** McKinsey workforce projections estimate the U.S. will need **130,000 additional trained electricians** between 2023 and 2030 just to keep pace with AI infrastructure expansion alone — on top of 240,000 more construction laborers and 150,000 more construction supervisors. That figure doesn't even include the competing demand from ordinary housing construction, hospitals, and renewable-energy projects, which are all pulling from the same pool of skilled electricians at the same time.

**What it's doing to pay, concretely:** data-center construction workers averaged **$81,800/year** in 2026 — about 32% above standard commercial-build pay. In one widely-reported example, three electricians under 30 working data-center jobs in Plano, Texas earned **between $240,000 and $280,000 annually**. This isn't the typical electrician's income (Section 10 has the honest, full-range numbers) — it's what happens at the sharp edge of an actual labor shortage, and it's worth understanding both the opportunity and why it's unusual.

**Why this trade specifically is AI-resistant:** unlike a lot of white-collar and even some skilled-labor work, physically installing, troubleshooting, and maintaining electrical systems inside a real building can't be done by a language model or a robot arm — someone has to be there, in the space, with hands, tools, and judgment. That's a genuinely different position to be in than most careers people are worried about right now, and it's a fair, honest thing to name directly with Shiloh: this is a trade AI is *creating* demand for, not eliminating.

**The honest catch, worth naming just as directly:** data-center electrical jobs are often project-based, not permanent. A single large facility might employ hundreds or thousands of electricians during an 18–24 month construction phase, but only 100–200 permanent staff once it's actually running. Workers chasing the top-dollar data-center wages often have to chain multiple projects together rather than counting on one steady job — which is exactly why the licensing ladder (Section 9) and a real, portable credential matter more than any single high-paying gig. A journeyman or master license travels with Shiloh; one job doesn't.

**The takeaway for Doc 07's Horizon Ladder:** this is a real, well-documented tailwind behind a trade that was already a strong Doc 10 candidate on its own merits (steady 9% national job growth, ~81,000 openings a year per BLS — Section 9). It's a legitimate reason to take this small bet seriously, not a reason to skip the fundamentals and chase the highest number in a headline.

## 9. The Licensing Ladder — From Apprentice to Master (Texas / TDLR)

Texas licenses electricians through the **Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR)**. This is the real, numbered ladder Doc 09 and Doc 10 already reference — laid out here in full so Shiloh can see the whole path at once.

| License tier | Requirement | Minimum age | Fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Electrical Apprentice** | Works under supervision of a master/journeyman/residential wireman while logging hours | 16 | $20 |
| **Residential Wireman** | 4,000 hours OJT, or an approved CTE program | — | $20 |
| **Journeyman Electrician** | 8,000 hours OJT under a master electrician, plus a licensing exam | — | $30 |
| **Journeyman Lineman** | 7,000 hours in a DOL-approved apprenticeship, or 3.5 years of utility experience, plus exam | — | $30 |
| **Master Electrician** | 12,000 hours OJT **and** at least 2 years holding a journeyman license, plus exam | — | $45 |
| **Electrical Contractor (business license)** | Held by the business itself, once a master electrician is ready to operate as a company | — | $110 |

**What this means on a timeline:** the apprentice-to-journeyman stretch is roughly **8,000 hours — about 4–5 years** of paid on-the-job training (this matches the national BLS figure in Section 8's companion data: ~2,000 paid hours/year of apprenticeship). Journeyman-to-master requires a further 12,000 hours *and* a minimum 2 years already holding the journeyman license — so master electrician, realistically, is a **7–10+ year total climb** from first apprentice registration. None of that is a bad thing; it's the same "earn while you learn" structure Doc 10 already flags as a real advantage over classroom-only trade school.

**Where this connects to Shiloh's actual timeline:** TDLR's apprentice registration has a **minimum age of 16**, so at 14, Shiloh can't yet register — but per Doc 09 §6 Option A, the years between now and then aren't dead time: dual-credit CTE coursework at a community college (welding, electrical, and similar Career Academy tracks) is available from 9th grade, contingent on hitting the college-readiness benchmarks Doc 09 already names. This course's math and reading work (Sections 3–5) is directly building toward that benchmark, whether or not electrical ends up being the trade Shiloh sticks with.

**Real entry point once the age gate opens:** the South Texas Electrical JATC (Joint Apprenticeship & Training Committee), already named in Doc 10 §3, is a documented, real registered-apprenticeship pipeline for exactly this trade.

## 10. From Journeyman to Founder — Owning Your Own Electrical Company

This is the part of the ladder most 14-year-olds never get shown, and it's the piece Thomas specifically asked to include: what actually happens *after* master electrician, and what it looks like to run a real company with employees.

**The honest income picture by stage** (national averages — Texas and high-demand markets can run higher, per Section 8):

| Stage | Typical experience | Typical annual pay |
|---|---|---|
| Apprentice | 0–4 years | ~$21,000–$56,000 (entry commonly $32,000–$38,000) |
| Journeyman | 4–8 years | ~$38,500–$104,500 (median around $68,000) |
| Master Electrician | 8–12+ years | ~$80,000 average; top 10% exceed $100,000 |
| Business owner (Electrical Contractor) | Master license + running a company | Personal draw commonly $60,000–$150,000; the business itself can gross $500,000+/year depending on workload, crew size, and expenses |

**What actually changes between "master electrician" and "company owner":** a master license lets someone legally pull permits and design electrical systems, but it doesn't automatically mean owning a business — that requires the separate $110 Electrical Contractor business license (Section 9), plus insurance, bonding, and the actual work of running a company: bidding jobs accurately, managing cash flow, and — the part that matters most for the "multiple employees" version of this — hiring, training, and being legally responsible for a crew of other electricians working under the business's license. That last piece is a real leadership and Systems & Self-Command skill, not just a technical one, which is exactly why Section 4's Week 10 pairs business math with the licensing content.

**Worth naming honestly:** plenty of licensed electricians choose to stay journeymen or masters working for someone else's company, and that's a completely legitimate, well-paying outcome on its own — owning a company isn't a required final step, it's an optional next door that opens once the license and the track record exist. One real, documented example worth showing Shiloh directly: a Gen Z electrician who launched his own contracting business at 21 crossed six figures in his first year running it — proof the "own company" door can open fast once the fundamentals and a license are actually in place, not just in the abstract.

**How this ties back to Doc 07:** this is a strong, concrete worked example for the Horizon Ladder (Doc 07 §4) — "apprentice at 16–18 → journeyman by early-to-mid 20s → master by late 20s/early 30s → own a company with a crew by mid-30s" is a realistic, well-documented 10-Year and 30-Year Vision shape, if this trade survives the small bet in Section 7.

## 11. Capstone — Build, Test, Pitch

**Required minimum, two parts:**

1. **The build:** Shiloh designs, builds, and tests a real (low-voltage, unenergized-current) circuit project on a trainer kit or breadboard — something with a switch, at least one series and one parallel section, and a measurable output (an LED, a small motor, a buzzer). He documents it with photos, a labeled diagram, and multimeter readings, walking Thomas through how it works using Section 3's vocabulary (voltage, current, resistance, Ohm's Law).
2. **The pitch:** a short, real business-plan pitch — "[Shiloh's Name] Electric" — using Section 9's licensing ladder and Section 10's real income numbers to lay out a realistic path from apprentice to owning a company with employees. This isn't a fantasy pitch deck; it should use the actual TDLR hour requirements and actual income ranges researched in this course, the same "use real numbers" standard Doc 14's costing exercises use.

**Stretch goal (optional):** if Week 11's informational interview or job shadow happened, fold a short clip or a direct quote from that real electrician into the pitch — grounding the plan in something Shiloh actually heard from someone doing the work, not just research.

Either version gets filmed if Shiloh wants it to double as Studio content (Section 13).
